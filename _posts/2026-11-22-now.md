### anticipating change

I asked my boss's boss to transfer me to Vancouver.
I think it would happen if it were up to her.
But she has to convince her boss and he might have to justify it to his because she in turn has a boss who has made it known publicly that he prefers employees work on site in specific cities that don't include Vancouver.

I'm in limbo.
Most likely my request will be denied.
And so, after working on this team and on this project for four and a half years, it might be time to leave.
I wanted to continue but now that I've been forced to find a new prospect I wonder whether it would be better to start afresh.
I'm not sure what I will choose if I am given both options.

If I stay on my current team, I can continue benefiting from the advantages I've accrued over the years.
Not least, my domain knowledge and my good reputation.
_(Give a man a reputation as an early riser and he can sleep 'til noon.)_
I can spare more time for my personal life and personal projects.
If I switch to the new team I've found, assuming they offer me the job, I will have to work on new projects with new people and impress new bosses.
For several months at least, I won't have much time or energy to put into extraprofessional activities.
And those, I suspect, are the ones that matter most.

My profession gives me satisfaction and enjoyment, but lately I feel demoralized.
Despite the effort and attention I give, the company may nonetheless toss out our project and me as well.
Ultimately we and what we do are crudely reduced before the bottom line.
It matters little if millions use or even depend on what we've made.
This of course would not change were I to transfer to another team or join another company.
Best I can do is position myself to benefit most without incurring too great a cost.
I've done that well for years.

As I contemplate my decision, I see some risks.
Atrophy is one.
Irrelevance another.
Third and most concerning is alienation.
Can I live a good life emotionally disconnected from my primary activity?
I want to spend most days creating or effecting or solving something.
Something of worth.
I need to expend energy and see it produce something.
My job therefore needs to take my energy in exchange for proportionate meaning or spare most of it so I can apply it where I will.

(Art is, in a way, the "easy" solution.
A piece of art – a painting, a song, a movie, an essay, a poem, whatever – claims its worth by existing.
It doesn't matter how many people experience it.
If it's good, it's good by itself, isn't it?
If so, could I maintain minimal, gainful employment and compensate with projects of my own?)

Unfortunately, even if I attain a life of lucrative returns on energy spent, I must still apportion some energy to maintenance of that life.
Cost of doing business.
So it is with one's "career," the narrative we create for the series of jobs one has over a lifetime.

Even if I care little about the industry of software engineering and have no ambition to make a name for myself in it, still I have to invest in skills that will buoy me, at least to stay afloat.
For that reason, even if I am offered my same job in Vancouver, it might be better to take the new job.
I could get experience of a different, more relevant kind and still, after a year or two, attain stability of the kind I've established at my current job.
