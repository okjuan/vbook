getting dismayed by corporate politics

Most the seven years I’ve worked at this megacorporation I’ve ignored “reorgs.” Who cares? The guy seven levels above me is a different guy now. So what?

So much, it turns out. In the second half of September, my eyes were opened to the petty and political motivations that determine the destiny of corporate products. No, it doesn’t matter what customers want, unless enough of them want something that will move the bottom line. What matters – if the earnest don’t or can’t intervene – is the retaining of power, the retaining of influence. I’m reminded of the passage in Bullshit Jobs in which David Graeber recounts the story of an employee who excitedly presented to upper management a substantial optimization of corporate process he had developed. The VPs whose departments would benefit from the proposal reacted not with gratitude or excitement but anger. What mattered most to them was having as many underlings as possible, no matter how needlessly they were employed.

In these last few weeks I’ve learned I must keep in contact with certain coworkers so that I might collect information about the fate of our projects. I’ve learned that in this environment too I must withold trust that decisions will be made for the right reasons. I regret the demise of worthy visions at the hands of the fearful and jealous.

delving deeper into municipal politics

On an evening in late September, before my weekly Wednesday night soccer game, I drove downtown to attend an event called the “Municipal Election Assembly.” I was one of two maybe three hundred people in attendance. Looking around the church I saw many of the bald and whitehaired heads that usually predominate events of local politics but I saw also enclaves and smatterings of “young people” like me, people under the age of forty.

At the foot of the altar facing back at the crowd sat two or three dozen people including the four mayoral candidates who had accepted to invitation. They were here to hear four proposals and to declare whether they committed to enacting them when and if they won the upcoming election. The organization that put on the event has no direct political or governmental power. It is a “grassroots coalition of community, faith, labour, and nonprofit organizations” and the influence they manage to exert is due to the sheer number of people they represent across the metropolitan area. These were their demands. Firstly, the new mayor would meet with the organization’s leaders within 30 days of taking office. The new mayor would remove barriers that prevent non-profit, faith, and community organizations from building housing on their own land. The new mayor would push the federal government for $2 billion in funding for deeply affordable homes. The new mayor would reduce traffic to improve transit and safety on Hastings Street in the Downtown East Side by piloting a new traffic pattern modeled on Toronto’s 2017 King Street Pilot. Finally, the new mayor would expand the city’s tree canopy by funding the first Youth Climate Corps program.

All four mayoral candidates in attendance agreed to all five demands. Their commitment does not mean they will succeed in each of these initiatives nor does it mean that they will even try. Politicians promise things all the time. Their agreement is in no way legally binding. But it is socially binding. If one of these six candidates becomes mayor, they and their party will have four specific ways to gain or lose the trust of tens of thousands of people all at once, whether by disappointing them directly or by betraying an organization that can sway elections by influencing how their members vote. Soft power is still power.

That same week I attended a smaller event in my neighborhood hosted in a gym at the beloved local community center. The same four mayoral candidates were joined this time by councilor candidates of six different parties and they all mingled and spoke with voters. After an hour the format was reshuffled into a Q&A panel where voters could ask a question and pick three candidates to answer. Here too the age of participants skewed older but was nonetheless attended by some “young people.”

This event was organized by a different grassroots organization with a drastically smaller scope of concern: a rectangle of 4.45km2 on the east side of the city. I approached a young couple I recognized from a similar event in January and they encouraged me to get involved with the organization. We exchanged emails and I made a meeting with the president of the “Area Council.” (It is a reasonable if unusual name that I assume they chose because the area is too big and too arbitrary to be called a neighborhood.) A week and a half later I went to his home and we sat in his living room and talked for an hour about decisions made already and decisions to be made that will transform the city for better and worse. He told me that a notable achievement in the organization’s history was when they helped cancel the freeway planned in the late 1960s that would have obliterated swaths of Strathcona and the Downtown Eastside. I’d been aware already of this significant moment in Vancouver’s history but when reading about it later I couldn’t help feel amazed and enormously thankful that the project was halted. This organization, like the other, has no direct power. And yet when it mobilizes and joins with an organized and willful public, it can shape reality itself.

My adventures continue online as well. I recently made vanvotes.ca in an effort to encourage uninformed citizens to educate themselves and vote in the municipal election. Bizarrely, while writing this, I asked google who was the creator of that site and ended up stumbling across northvanvotes.ca. What perplexed me is the similarity in URLs and in UI style, which is admittedly generic. I had not been so surprised when I had learned, after I made my quiz, that someone else had also made a quiz for the Vancouver municipal election. Luckily it complements mine.

Most recently, I joined a discord server for urbanists in the region. I requested to join months ago but their admins never followed up with me. I left it. But then the other day Russil – of morehousing.ca, who I had been emailing with and who had told me about canadianveggie.com’s quiz – asked me if I was on the discord. He told the admin about my lost request and now I’m on the discord.

thinking about what it takes to organize people

After I published vanvotes.ca I wanted to share it with as many people as possible. Reddit turned out to be a poor place to do so – to my frustration, moderators would not relent in judging my posts as self-promotion. I’ve relied mostly on group chats and social media to broadcast it and hope people use it.

Witnessing various events hosted by grassroots organizations and attended by small and large groups of people has stirred in me a desire to create something similar. Although I have no interest in managing people at work, I do fancy the prospect – at work and beyond – of managing coordinated efforts towards a coherent vision for which I am responsible. I have strong opinions and convictions, I have good social skills and communication skills, and I have a sometimes obsessive attention to detail but also a pragmatic tendency. This all sounds abstract and selfaggrandizing. But I know what I mean. And for the time being I am satisfied with the minor leadership roles I play in my life – senior software engineer, captain of a recreational soccer team, older brother,opinionated friend, to name a few. There are bigger roles in my future, father and parent the most obvious one. But maybe there are other big ones in my future, too.

thinking more conservatively about the future

There was a time in the late 2010s and the very early 2020s when opportunities seemed endless to me. Being a single young man moving to a new city on megacorporate dollar only amplified my feeling that a wealth of opportunities awaited outside my frontdoor if I felt so inclined to go out and engage them. I lacked nothing so obvious. And yet in my new daily life I was not so satisfied. I worked everyday in an office at the edge of a forest where began a wasteland of freeways, strip malls, car dealerships, and corporate campuses. Many other fortunate infant professionals like me arrived into similar circumstances and stepped gladly onto the conveyor belt of wealth accumulation and career progression. These were nice people but I found myself disinclined to meld into this social milieu.

Seven years on I am much happier. Although I work at the same company, life is much more my own. I work in the comfort and convenience of my own home, at a desk windowside. I deliver what is expected of me, at least, and retain the freedom to tend to personal and domestic tasks when they need tending. I work on a project I chose to join more than four years ago because I was interested in it. I work on the web frontend, despite the stigma the would claim it’s not “real software engineering.”

No doubt I’ve dabbled in audiobooks and investments, I’ve driven my career forward. But after these same seven years I don’t worry so much about keeping up with my peers. I realize I don’t care so much that my company isn’t cool, I care more for contentment at work, good compensation, and stability, now and in the future. All of a sudden I’m not so anxious about delving into a new world, a new company, a new experience. I like what I have. I want to stabilize it, sink stakes into the ground, and set up camp for a while. I yearn now not for breadth, but depth. I want to go deeper, not farther.

trying to say no

The first question is Do I want to do this? The next is Do I really not want to do this or am I just having pre-socialization anxiety? Maybe I need to change my attitude. But finally Am I pressuring myself to say yes out of social obligation in a way that is unfair to myself? How do I say No?

Molloy

Molloy (1951)

by Samuel Beckett

December 19 2024  (mod. January 11 2025)

#books #reading — Mentioned in what I'm doing now #7, what I'm doing now #31

I almost quit this book as soon as I began it. It launches the reader into the mind of an unnamed character lying on his deathbed and babbling on about basic details that he himself did not know or understand. He then proceeds to tell a mundane story in unfocused detail and with distracting metacommentary. Italo Calvino’s If on a winter’s night a traveler begins in a similar way. Perhaps Molloy was an inspiration.

The text was dense not only in style but also in line spacing. A paragraph break on the second page and then no more in sight. But I decided to push through. I motivated myself by striving to read as many pages as possible in one sitting. This worked well. Once I’d waded in far enough, giving up was much less tempting. It was satisfying to have made ample progress and, along the way, I’d begun enjoying the book. The experience was like going for a run. The first few minutes were uncomfortable and almost nauseating, but then I hit my stride and momentum carried me forth.

I read most of the book in two or three weeks. Then I let my attention drift towards other things. (Movies including The Godfather, YouTube videos, the audiobook

The World Beyond Your Head

, and the book The Neoliberal City.) Then I returned to it and read the last chunk over a few days. Soon after I finished the book I watched several reviews about it on YouTube and even ended up rewriting the Plot Summary section of the Wikipedia page because I found it unclear and partly contaminated with the writer’s interpretation that the two protagonists must be the same person. (I included my version of the plot summary at the end of this piece.)

I am not surprised to read on the Wikipedia page about Samuel Beckett that he is a contributor to Nonsense Literature and the Theatre of the Absurd. Molloy is strangely interesting despite its bizarreness and the apparent pointlessness of its story. One of its obvious qualities is the distinctiveness of each protagonist’s inner voice. You feel like you are inside their head, and the inscrutability of certain passages heightens that experience.

The novel is also very thoughtprovoking to consider in retrospect. There are strong hints that Molloy and Moran are the same person, but it also isn’t clear that they are. Moran never completes his mission of finding Molloy and instead begins to resemble him more and more. For its structure and the enigma at the heart of it, Molloy reminds me of David Lynch’s movie Mulholland Drive. Lynch tells the story of an envious actress who arranges the assassination of her former lover and consequently plunges into deeper despair and intolerable guilt. Lynch represents the irreparable shear that this trauma inflicts on the protagonist’s psyche by telling the story in two realities, a false sublimated one and then the real one. Perhaps Beckett is doing something similar in Molloy.

As interpreted by the psychoanalyst Dr. Olga Cox Cameron, the first part reproduces Freudian Dream Logic by obsessively approaching objects of trauma with sublimated meanings and then fleeing further into fantasy when they become too intolerable to consider.

Plot Summary

The novel is about two characters and is divided into two parts. Each part is an internal monologue, the first of Molloy and the second of Jacques Moran.

Part One consists of two paragraphs: the first spans about two pages and the second more than eighty. The book opens with the narrator stating that he lives in his mother’s room, but does not remember how he arrived there or when his mother died. In this room he writes and every Sunday a man visits to pick up what he has written and bring back what he had taken last week “marked with signs” that Molloy never cares to read. Molloy writes to “speak of the things that are left, say [his] goodbyes, finish dying”. In the second paragraph, which comprises most of Part One, Molloy recounts a journey he took supposedly to find his mother. Throughout the journey, he struggles to remember that his aim is to meet his mother and finds himself endlessly sidetracked and delayed. Molloy suffers from severe physical disabilities, and so relies on his bicycle and his crutches for mobility. At one point, he gets momentarily arrested for resting on his bicycle in a lewd way. It is when speaking to the police in the station that we finally learn his name, which suddenly comes to him and he cries out while being questioned. Soon after his release, Molloy wanders through town and accidentally kills an old dog by running over it with his bicycle. A vicious mob starts forming, but then Molloy is rescued by the dog’s owner who forgives him and takes him into her home. He is fed, bathed, clothed, and allowed to live on the premises for free. Despite this, Molloy resents the woman – whose name he can’t quite remember: “a Mrs Loy… or Lousse, I forget, Christian name something like Sophie” – and after a long time finally musters the determination to leave, without his bicycle. Soon afterwards, he finds a place in an alley to harm himself, which brings him satisfaction. He remembers an old woman who used to compensate him for having intercourse with her and with whom he considers, absurdly, to have known true love. Molloy then journeys out of town towards the shore, having again forgotten his objective of finding his mother. He tells of living by the ocean for a while and describes in excruciating detail his process of circulating sixteens “sucking stones” across his four pockets to maximize the amount of time between which each stone is sucked. Eventually he leaves the shore and journeys through the woods. There, he encounters a man who appears to need help but Molloy ignores him. The man insists until Molloy becomes suddenly violent, beating the man until he stops moving. Soon afterwards, Molloy makes it out of the woods and Part One abruptly ends.

Part Two is narrated by a private detective by the name of Jacques Moran, who is assigned the task of tracking down Molloy and carrying out specific instructions. The task is given to him by a man named Gaber, who is apparently his supervisor and takes order from a higher boss named Youdi. This narrative (Part Two) begins:

It is midnight. The rain is beating on the windows.

It becomes immediately apparent that Moran is a cruel and arbitrarily abusive father. He sets out on his journey to find Molloy and forces his son Jacques to come with him. They wander across the countryside, increasingly bogged down by the weather, decreasing supplies of food and Moran’s suddenly failing body. He sends his son to purchase a bicycle and while his son is gone, Moran encounters an old man with a large, but light walking stick. Later, Moran is confronted by another man, who he murders in a sudden violent outburst reminiscent of Molloy and hides his body in the forest. After a few days, the son returns with a bicycle and is aghast at his father’s appearance. Immediately, Moran resumes his senseless, violent treatment of his son and the two continue on their journey to Bally. In the outskirts of Bally, they encounter a shepherd with his dog and sheep. For some reason, Moran is transfixed and enchanted. In the morning, Moran wakes to find that his son has fled with the bicycle and most of the money. Moran takes the news with strange warmth and optimism. Moran finishes his provisions and gives up on his journey. He waits for starvation to kill him. Every night, he crawls out of his camp to look at the lights of Bally in the distance and “laughs” strenuously at the sight of it. One night, Moran finds Gaber outside his camp. They have an absurd exchange in which Moran pleads with Gaber to divulge what Youdi said. Gaber disappears quickly thereafter. Moran then embarks on what he understands to be his new assignment, returning home. He limps and drags himself through the countryside through the fall and winter at an excruciatingly slow pace and poses bizarre questions to himself about biblical topics and matters of the church. His ramblings begin to resemble Molloy’s from Part One. During his journey home, he is confronted by an angry farmer who demands to know what he is doing on his land. Feeling that preferred strategy of violence is too risky, Moran makes up a story about doing a pilgrimage and requests a tea to send the farmer away so that he can escape. When Moran finally arrives home, several months later, he laments the death of his bees and hens, which were left out all winter. He settles back into his home and begins to use crutches, like Molloy. Not for the first time, Moran mentions a “voice” that has been gradually speaking to him more and more, and which he claims to understand better now. The novel ends with Moran sitting down to write the report that his boss Youdi, his supervisor Gaber, and the mysterious voice keep demanding he write:

Then I went back into the house and wrote, It is midnight. The rain is beating on the windows. It was not midnight. It was not raining.
