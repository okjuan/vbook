Life

Preparing green card papers for my wife, Z. We probably won’t get them until early 2025. Sigh.

Enjoying the fall. Except for the short days. Sun sets at 4:30pm nowadays. Blehj.

Playing soccer. I’ve noticed in the last several months that I’m scoring more goals from longer range, 20+ yards away from goal. I’m delighted that it’s turning out to be one of my strengths in the game. I’d gotten used to thinking that headers and penalties were my only goal-scoring strengths. (And, actually, I’ve gotten worse at penalties in the last several years.) I think I’m reaping the benefits of the hours I spent in my late teens (and perhaps early twenties) practicing long-range shooting.

Losing weight. I’ve lost ~11lbs in the last 5 months, an average of 2.2lbs per month. If I can keep up that rate, I’ll reach my goal weight of 189lbs in 13.6 months. Geez, that’s January 2025. Sounds like early 2025 will be cathartic time for me.

I’ve noticed my attitude towards money has shifted recently. For years, I’ve wanted to keep open the option of taking a major career risk, so I wanted to save as much as possible. But nowadays, I’m pretty happy and could see myself staying at Microsoft for a few more years, at least. I am fortunate that money is not a scarce resource for me and I can use it to save time and make my life better. I still try not to overspend, but I don’t let it dominate my decisions as much anymore.

Reading

I just finished 2 books: The Courage to be Disliked by Ichiro Kishimi and Fumitake Koga and Into the Abyss: A Neuropsychiatrist’s Notes on Troubled Minds by Anthony David. The Courage was thought-provoking and written in a novel form. See Derek Sivers’ notes on it. Into the Abyss was full of fascinating cases and some good insight.

Recently read Anthony Lane’s hilarious New Yorker article, Can Happiness Be Taught? It’s a review of recent best-seller Build the Life You Want: The Art and Science of Getting Happier, written by a Professor of Management Practice at Harvard Business School and sponsored by Oprah Winfrey. One of my favorite bits:

…imperative reigns supreme. “Start by working on your toughness.” No sweat. “Take your grand vision of improvement and humble ambition to be part of it in a specific way and execute accordingly.” Check. “Rebel against your shame.” Done. “Widen your conflict-resolution repertoire.” Ka-pow! “Treat your walks, prayer time, and gym sessions as if they were meetings with the president.” Which President? “Journal your experiences and feelings over the course of the day.” Since when did “journal” turn into a transitive verb? “Dig into the extensive and growing technology and literature on mindfulness.” Sorry, I was miles away, what? Above all, “Remember: You are your own CEO.” Holy moly. Do I have to wear a suit to brush my teeth? Is my dog a shareholder? Were last year’s migraines tax-deductible? Can I be fired by me?

anticipating change

I asked my boss’s boss to transfer me to Vancouver. I think it would happen if it were up to her. But she has to convince her boss and he might have to justify it to his because she in turn has a boss who has made it known publicly that he prefers employees work on site in specific cities that don’t include Vancouver.

I’m in limbo. Most likely my request will be denied. And so, after working on this team and on this project for four and a half years, it might be time to leave. I wanted to continue but now that I’ve been forced to find a new prospect I wonder whether it would be better to start afresh. I’m not sure what I will choose if I am given both options.

If I stay on my current team, I can continue benefiting from the advantages I’ve accrued over the years. Not least, my domain knowledge and my good reputation. (Give a man a reputation as an early riser and he can sleep ‘til noon.) I can spare more time for my personal life and personal projects. If I switch to the new team I’ve found, assuming they offer me the job, I will have to work on new projects with new people and impress new bosses. For several months at least, I won’t have much time or energy to put into extraprofessional activities. And those, I suspect, are the ones that matter most.

My profession gives me satisfaction and enjoyment, but lately I feel demoralized. Despite the effort and attention I give, the company may nonetheless toss out our project and me as well. Ultimately we and what we do are crudely reduced before the bottom line. It matters little if millions use or even depend on what we’ve made. This of course would not change were I to transfer to another team or join another company. Best I can do is position myself to benefit most without incurring too great a cost. I’ve done that well for years.

As I contemplate my decision, I see some risks. Atrophy is one. Irrelevance another. Third and most concerning is alienation. Can I live a good life emotionally disconnected from my primary activity? I want to spend most days creating or effecting or solving something. Something of worth. I need to expend energy and see it produce something. My job therefore needs to take my energy in exchange for proportionate meaning or spare most of it so I can apply it where I will.

(Art is, in a way, the “easy” solution. A piece of art – a painting, a song, a movie, an essay, a poem, whatever – claims its worth by existing. It doesn’t matter how many people experience it. If it’s good, it’s good by itself, isn’t it? If so, could I maintain minimal, gainful employment and compensate with projects of my own?)

Unfortunately, even if I attain a life of lucrative returns on energy spent, I must still apportion some energy to maintenance of that life. Cost of doing business. So it is with one’s “career,” the narrative we create for the series of jobs one has over a lifetime.

Even if I care little about the industry of software engineering and have no ambition to make a name for myself in it, still I have to invest in skills that will buoy me, at least to stay afloat. For that reason, even if I am offered my same job in Vancouver, it might be better to take the new job. I could get experience of a different, more relevant kind and still, after a year or two, attain stability of the kind I’ve established at my current job.
